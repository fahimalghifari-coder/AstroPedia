The Big Bang Theory

The scientific model describing the origin and evolution of the universe.

Figure 1.1 — Artistic illustration of the Big Bang singularity expansion and early cosmic ignition.

1. General Statement

The big bang theory of the universe starts with a single point which rapidly expanded and slowly evolved into the cosmos we see today. Some 13.8 billion years ago, the universe was a dense, tremendously hot (too hot for the existence of atoms), extremely tiny point that rapidly surged outward in all directions. For a fraction of a second, the universe expanded faster than the speed of light.

2. Scientific Evidence (NASA Research)

The validity of the Big Bang theory is strongly supported by three major pillars of observational evidence studied by space agencies like NASA. First, the expansion of the universe, observed by Edwin Hubble, showing galaxies moving away from each other. Second, the existence of the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) radiation, which serves as the ancient thermal fossil echo from the hot primeval phase. Third, the observed abundance of light elements such as hydrogen, helium, and lithium, precisely matching the theoretical predictions of early cosmic nucleosynthesis.

3. Cosmic Description

Following the cooling period after the Big Bang, gravity began pulling cosmic materials together across hundreds of millions of years, giving birth to magnificent galactic structures. A prominent example of this evolutionary result is a beautiful massive ancient spiral galaxy like our Milky Way. These stellar systems act as vast cosmic ecosystems that bind trillions of solar systems, gas clouds, and dark matter into enduring celestial architectures.

Black Holes

The mysterious regions of space with gravitational fields so strong nothing can escape.

Figure 2.1 — Scientific visualization of an accretion disk wrapping around an invisible black hole.

1. General Statement

A black hole is a place in space where gravity pulls so much that even light can not get out. The gravity is so strong because matter has been squeezed into a tiny space. This can happen when a star is dying. Because no light can get out, people can't see black holes. They are invisible. Space telescopes with special tools can help find black holes.

2. Scientific Evidence & Observation (NASA)

Although direct light cannot escape from a black hole, space agencies like NASA study them through indirect observation. When matter is pulled inward by intense gravity, it forms a bright spinning accretion disk that emits high-energy X-rays. Furthermore, space telescopes monitor the orbital movements of nearby stars and gas clouds to calculate the massive gravitational pull exerted by hidden black holes at the center of galaxies.

3. Cosmic Description

Near the boundary of these enigmatic cosmic regions lies **a dark mysterious massive gravitational abyss** that shapes the dynamics of entire star systems. These invisible gravitational powerhouses warp space-time profoundly, creating some of the most awe-inspiring yet elusive phenomena in our universe.
